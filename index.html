The Ocean Is Vast. So How Did Pirates Find Anyone?

I How Far Can You See?

The first surprise is how far a lookout can actually see. From a crow's nest 100 feet above the waterline, the geometric horizon is nearly 12 nautical miles away. But a pirate's lookout wasn't scanning for the horizon. He was scanning for masts — and a merchant ship's rigging rises 80 feet or more above the water, visible long before the hull clears the curvature of the Earth. Combine both heights with the formula navigators have used for centuries, and one pair of eyes can detect a ship over 23 nautical miles away.

From a crow's nest, you're watching 1,660 square nautical miles of ocean. That's larger than Rhode Island.

II The Ribbon

Now set that lookout in motion. A pirate ship under sail at 5 knots covers 120 nautical miles per day. The detection circle drags forward, sweeping a ribbon of ocean 40 miles wide and 120 long — nearly 4,800 square nautical miles of surveilled sea every day.

But the critical insight isn't the area — it's the ratio. Trade routes don't spread evenly across the ocean. They compress into straits, passages, and coastal channels, wherever geography, wind, and current force ships through the same water.

The Windward Passage is 43 nautical miles wide. A pirate's lookout can see 40 miles across. One ship nearly covers the entire strait.

III The Funnel

Geography doesn't just compress lanes — it creates funnels. The Windward Passage between Cuba and Hispaniola, the Strait of Malacca, the mouth of the Red Sea: every era's piracy hotspot sits at a chokepoint where trade is forced through predictable corridors. Bartholomew Roberts, the most successful pirate of the Golden Age, didn't roam the Atlantic at random. He positioned himself where the shipping lanes converged — and took over 400 vessels in three years.

IV The Math

Now put it all together. Given a detection width, a lane width, and a daily traffic rate, the probability of encounter is not guesswork — it is arithmetic. Each day is an independent trial. But the cumulative probability — the chance of at least one encounter — ratchets toward certainty faster than intuition expects.

At a 43-mile chokepoint with two ships per day, the question is never whether. It is how many days.

At the Windward Passage with 2 ships per day, a pirate has a 99% chance of encountering a target within 3 days. The ocean is big — but the lanes are narrow.

V The Arms Race

Sections I–IV prove piracy works. The geometry guarantees it. So why didn't piracy destroy all maritime trade? Because merchants and empires fought back — and the defenses they invented map directly onto the parameters above. Spain's Flota de Indias gathered ships into armed convoys starting in the 1560s, reducing targets per day but adding costly waiting time. Merchants armed their vessels, sacrificing cargo space for cannon. Some captains abandoned the chokepoints entirely, sailing longer routes through open water. And when England dispatched Woodes Rogers to Nassau in 1718 with Royal Navy warships and pardons for surrendering pirates, it marked the beginning of the end.

The result is a Nash equilibrium: perfect safety costs more than the piracy it prevents. Toggle defenses below and watch the economics play out.

Without defense, pirates capture a significant fraction of all traffic. This is the baseline — the price of unprotected trade.

The ocean is vast — but trade is predictable, lookouts see far, and geography is a funnel. Pirates didn't find ships by luck. They found them by math — and the same math that guaranteed encounters eventually priced piracy out of business.
